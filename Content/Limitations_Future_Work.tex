\chapter{Limitations and Future Work}
\label{chap:limitations-future}

This chapter separates limitations and future work from the final conclusion so that the scope of the thesis is explicit. The experiments are useful because they reveal a clear gap between representation robustness and downstream robustness. They are not a final benchmark for all foundation vision models. Understanding what the thesis does not show is necessary for using its results correctly.

\section{Architectural Scope}

The evaluated encoders are from the \DINO family. This is appropriate because \DINO and \DINOv are strong self-supervised vision encoders with clear relevance to downstream transfer. However, the conclusion should not be read as an exhaustive statement about every possible SSL architecture. Masked autoencoders, contrastive ResNets, hybrid ConvNet--ViT models, and newer multimodal encoders could show different quantitative behavior.

The robust encoder is also limited to the available \DeACL-tuned \DINO ViT-B/16. This creates an asymmetry: the strongest clean encoders in the tables are \DINOv models, while the robust row is a \DINO model. The thesis handles this by comparing \DeACL primarily to its matching standard \DINO row. Still, future work should robustly fine-tune the stronger \DINOv encoders and test whether scale or newer pre-training changes the transfer pattern.

Architecture may matter in several ways. Patch size affects spatial granularity. Token mixing affects how local perturbations propagate. Pre-training data affects distribution alignment. Larger models may have more stable features or simply more exploitable directions. The current results motivate these questions but do not answer them fully.

\section{Task Scope}

The selected tasks cover global classification, semantic dense prediction, and geometric dense prediction. This is a meaningful set, but many downstream tasks remain unevaluated. Object detection, instance segmentation, panoptic segmentation, optical flow, pose estimation, visual correspondence, retrieval, video understanding, and medical-image analysis could all expose different robustness properties.

Detection is especially interesting because it combines classification and localization. A representation-level defense might protect class predictions while leaving bounding boxes fragile. Optical flow and correspondence would test motion or matching rather than static semantics. Medical tasks would test distribution shift and high-stakes dense outputs. These tasks are natural extensions of the thesis.

A larger benchmark should also include multiple heads per task. The current heads are lightweight to isolate the encoder. Deployed systems may use stronger decoders, feature pyramids, or end-to-end fine-tuning. Such systems may be more accurate cleanly and could be more or less robust. Future work should compare lightweight heads with stronger task-specific heads while keeping the encoder fixed.

\section{Attack Scope}

The thesis uses first-order white-box $\ell_\infty$ attacks. This is a standard and important threat model, but it is not complete. Other norms such as $\ell_2$ and $\ell_1$ produce different perturbation geometries. Spatial transformations can preserve pixel values while moving content. Patch attacks can be localized but visible. Corruption robustness tests common non-adversarial shifts such as blur, noise, compression, and weather.

Physical-world attacks are also outside scope. A perturbation printed on a sign, projected on a surface, or introduced through camera effects has constraints that differ from digital $\ell_\infty$ perturbations. Dense tasks such as segmentation and depth may be especially sensitive to physical conditions. A deployed robustness claim should therefore include physical or corruption evaluations when relevant.

Black-box attacks are another extension. A public foundation encoder may be accessible only through an API or through downstream outputs. Transfer attacks from surrogate models can still be effective. Query-based attacks can estimate gradients. The white-box results in this thesis are a necessary baseline, but black-box robustness remains an open deployment question.

\section{Defense Scope}

\DeACL is the only robustification method evaluated in depth. It is a strong choice because it is decoupled, efficient, and directly represented in the supplied resources. Nevertheless, future defenses may transfer better. A method that includes dense-task adversarial objectives, patch-token consistency, multi-head sampling, or certified bounds could improve downstream robustness.

One promising direction is multi-task robust fine-tuning. Instead of aligning only representations, the robust encoder could be trained with a mixture of representation, classification, segmentation, and depth losses. The difficulty is avoiding overfitting to a small set of heads. A foundation encoder should support unknown future tasks, so the training tasks must be diverse enough to teach general stability.

Another direction is adaptor-aware encoder training. During robust fine-tuning, the method could sample simple synthetic adaptors or linearizations and enforce robustness through them. This would sit between pure representation alignment and full task-specific adversarial training. It might improve transfer while preserving some task agnosticism.

A third direction is robust token geometry. Dense tasks rely on patch tokens. Robust training could explicitly constrain local token neighborhoods, attention maps, or multi-scale feature consistency. Such constraints may help segmentation and depth more than global representation alignment alone.

\section{Evaluation Reporting}

Future papers should report robustness matrices. At minimum, a robust foundation vision paper should include rows for several downstream tasks and columns for clean performance, representation attack, and task attack. The attack budget, steps, random starts, loss, target representation, and downstream head should be specified. Without these details, results are hard to compare.

Reporting should also include clean--robust trade-off curves. A single checkpoint can hide the cost of robustification. The supplied fine-tuning figures already point in this direction. Showing how clean and attacked metrics evolve over epochs helps readers decide whether a method is improving general robustness or merely optimizing one attacked metric.

Finally, papers should avoid vague statements such as ``the encoder is robust'' unless the scope is explicit. Better statements are ``the encoder is robust to representation-distance attacks under this budget'' or ``the segmentation pipeline remains robust under \SegPGD.'' This language may be longer, but it is scientifically clearer.

\section{Possible Extensions of This Thesis}

The most direct extension is experimental. Re-run the full benchmark with additional encoders and defenses. Add robust \DINOv rows. Add MAE and contrastive encoders. Add stronger segmentation and depth heads. Evaluate multiple perturbation budgets and norms. This would turn the thesis protocol into a larger benchmark.

A second extension is analytical. Study why \DeACL transfer fails. Possible approaches include measuring representation drift, token-level sensitivity, gradient alignment between \EmbedAttack and task losses, and layer-wise robustness. If task gradients point in directions not constrained by representation alignment, that would explain the empirical gap more directly.

A third extension is methodological. Develop an attack that is still downstream-agnostic but better predicts downstream failures. For example, an attack could maximize changes in multiple layers, patch-token covariance, or local feature neighborhoods. Such an attack might serve as a stronger proxy for unknown downstream tasks.

A fourth extension is practical. Build a reporting toolkit that takes an encoder and a set of lightweight heads, runs clean and adversarial evaluations, and outputs the task-and-attack matrix advocated in this thesis. Such a tool would make robust foundation-model evaluation easier to reproduce.

\section{Ethical and Deployment Considerations}

The thesis studies attacks in order to improve evaluation, not to encourage misuse. Adversarial robustness research has dual-use aspects: the same methods that reveal vulnerabilities could be used to exploit deployed systems. In this setting, the attacks are standard white-box evaluation tools, and the reported results are aggregate metrics, not instructions for attacking a particular real-world deployment.

For safety-critical applications, the main ethical lesson is caution. A model should not be deployed with a broad robustness claim based on narrow evaluation. If a segmentation model is used in driving, medical imaging, or robotics, it should be attacked at the segmentation interface. If a depth model informs navigation, it should be attacked at the depth interface. Robustness claims should be matched to the risks of the actual application.

The clean--robust trade-off also has ethical implications. Reducing clean performance can harm users even if robustness improves under one threat model. Deployment decisions should consider both ordinary error rates and adversarial failure modes. Transparent reporting helps stakeholders make those trade-offs explicitly.

\section{Summary}

The limitations do not weaken the central result; they define it. Under the evaluated white-box $\ell_\infty$ setting, with \DINO-family encoders and \DeACL robust fine-tuning, representation-level robustness does not reliably transfer to downstream task robustness. Future work should expand architectures, tasks, attacks, defenses, and reporting tools. The thesis provides a focused starting point and a clear evaluation philosophy for that work.
